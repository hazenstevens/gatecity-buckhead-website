Walking with Jesus on the Emmaus Road

By Hazen Stevens · November 18, 2026

Five habits of a disciple, found on one seven-mile walk. Key text: Luke 24:13–35.

Some of the most important conversations of our lives happen while we are walking somewhere else. Most mornings I walk back and forth along my driveway for about a mile and talk with Jesus. It takes about twenty minutes. Over one of the hardest years of my life, that short walk has quietly become one of the anchors of my life with God. So it moves me that on the very first Easter, the risen Christ chose to reveal himself to two of his followers not in the temple or the upper room, but on a dusty road, at the end of a long and disappointing day.

That same day two of Jesus’ followers were walking to the village of Emmaus, seven miles from Jerusalem. As they walked along they were talking about everything that had happened. As they talked and discussed these things, Jesus himself suddenly came and began walking with them. But God kept them from recognizing him.
Luke 24:13–16 (NLT)

Read slowly, this story becomes more than a resurrection account. It becomes a prophetic picture of who we are called to be as disciples. In the space of one evening, these two friends live out every habit of a life that walks closely with Jesus.

Two friends on the road home

It is the evening of the resurrection. Two disciples are walking seven miles from Jerusalem to Emmaus, roughly a two-hour journey on foot. They are confused and grieving. Everything they had hoped for seems to have died on a Roman cross.

One of them is named Cleopas. The other is never named. For most of my life I assumed these were two men, but the text never says so. Many commentators have suggested the unnamed companion may have been Cleopas’s wife, perhaps the Mary “the wife of Clopas” who stood at the cross (John 19:25). They are traveling together, and they share a home at the end of the road. I love that possibility, because all of us have companions on the journey. For me, that companion is my wife, Hannah.

The name Cleopas likely means “glory of the father.” I find it meaningful that he is the one Scripture names. If we take this story as a prophetic picture, it whispers something to us: as we walk with Jesus, the glory of the Father is revealed.

Notice, too, who makes the first move. The disciples are not searching for Jesus. Jesus himself comes near and begins walking with them. From the garden, where “the LORD God” walked “when the cool evening breezes were blowing” and called out, “Where are you?” (Genesis 3:8–9, NLT), to this dusty road outside Jerusalem, our God is the God who pursues us.

1. Prayer: they walked and talked with Jesus

The whole story begins in conversation. Jesus asks them what they are discussing, and they pour out their grief, their confusion and their disappointed hopes. They do not yet know who he is, and yet they are already talking with him.

This is the heart of prayer. Before it is a discipline, it is a conversation with the One who has drawn near. Many of us carry an idea that prayer has to be polished or long to count. The Emmaus Road invites us to something simpler: honest conversation with Jesus on the road we are already walking, even before we fully recognize him in it.

For me, the practice that has made the biggest difference is meeting God first, before the day pulls me in other directions. The psalmist put it this way:

O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee.
Psalm 63:1 (KJV)

Responsibility has a way of crowding that ambition, and I have had to fight for it as life has grown fuller. But my day is different when it begins with him, and I try to close it the same way, even if it is just a quiet word with him in my heart. If mornings are not possible in your season, redeem the time you have. A commute, a walk or a shower can become a sanctuary. The point is not the clock. The point is the conversation.

2. Serving: they welcomed a stranger in

By this time they were nearing Emmaus and the end of their journey. Jesus acted as if he were going on, but they begged him, “Stay the night with us, since it is getting late.” So he went home with them.
Luke 24:28–29 (NLT)

Tired and heartbroken as they are, these two still notice a traveler who has nowhere to stay as night falls. They open their door. They urge him to come in.

Serving often begins exactly here: with an open door. It is the willingness to put someone else’s need in front of our own fatigue. When we are honest, the things that keep us from serving are usually internal. Sometimes it is a bit of selfishness that does not want to sacrifice. Sometimes we simply do not know where to start, or we are unsure how God has gifted us. The Emmaus disciples did not have a ministry title. They had a home and a willingness to share it, and that was enough to make room for Jesus.

3. Generosity: they shared their table

As they sat down to eat, he took the bread and blessed it. Then he broke it and gave it to them. Suddenly, their eyes were opened, and they recognized him. And at that moment he disappeared!
Luke 24:30–31 (NLT)

They shared their food and their table, and it was around that table, as the bread was broken, that their eyes were opened. Generosity does more than meet a need. It creates a place where Jesus is made known.

Many of us are generous in the moment, when we are moved. The deeper invitation is to make generosity a settled posture of the heart. Years ago I made a simple decision that helped me: whenever someone asks me for help, I will pray, and I will give something, even if it is small. Even when I visit another church and an offering is received, I give. That one practice keeps my hands open instead of closed. It resets the default setting of my heart from protecting to sharing.

4. Formation: their hearts burned together

They said to each other, “Didn’t our hearts burn within us as he talked with us on the road and explained the Scriptures to us?”
Luke 24:32 (NLT)

Earlier, Luke tells us that “Jesus took them through the writings of Moses and all the prophets, explaining from all the Scriptures the things concerning himself” (Luke 24:27, NLT). The Word of God, opened by the living Word, set their hearts on fire.

Look closely at their language. They do not say “my heart.” They say “our hearts.” This formative encounter was something they shared. We are not meant to burn alone. Our inner lives are formed in community, around the Word, with others who are walking the same road.

Jesus said, “People do not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God” (Matthew 4:4, NLT). When we pray “give us this day our daily bread,” we can ask for that nourishment too. Two simple practices have helped me and many in our community:

Have a plan, and finish it. Many of us start in Genesis and quit somewhere in Leviticus. Like paying off your smallest debt first to build momentum, start with something you can complete. Read the Gospel of Mark, two chapters a day, and you will finish in about two weeks. Or read Colossians, a beautiful four-chapter portrait of Jesus. Then take the next step. The satisfaction of finishing builds a habit that lasts.

Find your people. Ask yourself honestly: do I have a consistent group where I talk about spiritual things? For me, one of those groups is my kids. Every morning on the way to school we read a devotional and talk about it together. Another is our Wednesday Formation Nights. If your group is your family, ask when your family altar happens. If you do not yet have a group, that may be the very next step God is inviting you to take.

5. Reach: they went back out into the dark

And within the hour they were on their way back to Jerusalem. There they found the eleven disciples and the others who had gathered with them, who said, “The Lord has really risen! He appeared to Peter.” Then the two from Emmaus told their story of how Jesus had appeared to them as they were walking along the road, and how they had recognized him as he was breaking the bread.
Luke 24:33–35 (NLT)

I love that this story begins with their hearts downcast. When Jesus asked what they were discussing, “They stopped short, sadness written across their faces” (Luke 24:17, NLT). Jesus met them right there in their sorrow and asked them about it. But something radical happened once they recognized him in the breaking of the bread. Something moved in them, and it led them to do something that would have seemed absurd only an hour earlier.

They had told Jesus it was getting late, too late to keep traveling. Yet once they had seen him, they did not wait for morning. In my mind, they leapt up from the table, dashed out the door and walked the same seven miles back to Jerusalem, in the dark, so they could tell the others he is alive. The road was no safer that night than it had been an hour before. What had changed was them.

Same road. New vision. Their grief had turned to joy, and joy cannot keep quiet.

That willingness to be uncomfortable, to go into dark places, is at the heart of reaching others. Following Jesus will lead us out of comfortable rooms and into conversations, neighborhoods and relationships we might naturally avoid. We go not because it is easy, but because we have seen him, and we cannot keep it to ourselves.

I have seen this in my own life. Following Jesus has taken me to places I would never have chosen on my own. I have been in parts of Haiti where we heard voodoo drums playing through the night. I have helped bring medical care and the love of Jesus to some of the poorest people I have ever met, in one of the largest slums in East Africa. And some of the darkest places have been right here in our own city. We have ministered to men on their way into a strip club, and sent women from our team inside to minister the love of God to the women taking their clothes off for money. They went to tell them they were loved and valued, without condemnation, accusation or judgment. Again and again I have watched God bring light into dark places through simple acts of kindness, practical care and his supernatural power. The light always shines brightest in the darkest places.

It almost always begins with prayer. It is hard to reach people we are not praying for. So ask the Lord for two or three people, or even a people, to carry in prayer by name until you see him at work in their lives. I regularly pray for a family member who is not yet walking with Jesus, and lately I have seen small signs of God moving in their life. Prayer is how God begins to open doors, and over time we begin to see the fruit.

But prayer is only the beginning. We also have to be willing to walk through the doors God opens: to extend the invitation, to share our story, to ask someone to come with us, and to get uncomfortable ourselves. The Emmaus disciples did not stay at the table and simply hope the others would hear. They got up and went into the darkness of night.

Your next step on the road

Prayer, serving, generosity, formation and reach. Five habits, all on one road, all lived by two ordinary disciples on the evening their sorrow turned to joy. Jesus is still drawing near to people on the road. He is still opening the Scriptures, still breaking bread, still sending his people out with good news.

So take a moment with these questions:

  • Which of these five habits is strongest in your life right now?
  • Which one is Jesus inviting you to grow in this season?
  • What is one small, concrete step you could take this week?

A blessing for your road

May the risen Jesus walk beside you on every road you travel. When your heart is heavy, may he draw near. May he open the Scriptures to you until your heart burns within you. May your table be open and your eyes be opened as he breaks the bread. And when he sends you out, even into the dark, may you run with joy to tell others: the Lord is risen indeed. In Jesus’ name, amen.
